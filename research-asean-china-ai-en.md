# ASEAN–China AI Cooperation: Where the ASEAN Foundation Can Contribute

14 October 2026

## Summary

**Recommendation**: The ASEAN Foundation's place in ASEAN–China AI cooperation lies in the capabilities of 'people and businesses that use AI', and above all in **the stage at which completed training translates into actual use**. The MSME track is the first priority for this visit. The Foundation should open with the CIIS roundtable remarks (A3) and put forward, as the direction of its contribution, the three points that the Singapore local implementing partner (LIP) set out in the key summaries of its final report: linking training to implementation, building AI-bilingual teams, and measuring responsible use and business outcomes. The teacher track (C1) is structurally important but remains a conditional task, to be pursued after confirming whether China's Smart Education includes teachers and reporting to SOM-ED.

1. **ASEAN–China AI cooperation has broadened to cover almost every AI layer.** It now spans governance (the ministerial roundtable on AI and the September 2026 initiative on AI ethics and safety cooperation for ASEAN), industrial application (the China–ASEAN AI Industry Innovation Centre (Beijing), the China–ASEAN AI Application Cooperation Centre (Nanning) and a casebook of 31 cases), talent (the Ministry of Science and Technology's three-year plan and training at the Innovation Centre; ASEAN's share of the 5,000 AI training sessions and seminars China has offered to developing countries as a whole is not confirmed) and language and data (the Lao national language model and the ASEAN AI open-source community). The talent programmes, however, are presented only in terms of numbers and target groups; how use after training is supported and measured has not been made public.
2. **The gaps are 'the layer of AI users' and the stage 'after training'.** For teachers, citizens and MSMEs, there is only AI education offered by Chinese companies and platforms, together with Smart Education, whose target groups have not been disclosed; no ASEAN-led, technology-neutral AI capability programme is linked to the cooperation. Among students, 72.4 per cent have never taken an AI course; among educators, 70.5 per cent. The Singapore Convening found that even in Singapore, with its robust policy and support environment, support tends to break off between awareness, experimentation, implementation and scaling. Separately, IMDA figures put Singapore's 2025 AI adoption rate at 23.4 per cent for SMEs and 70.4 per cent for other firms (the Convening did not address adoption rates by firm size).
3. **The Foundation has assets for this stage, and a partner's recommendations set out the role it would play (subject to agreement).** The assets are a delivery network (more than 3,000 Master Trainers and local partners) and an evidence base (the AI readiness research and the Convening records); the role is the one the Singapore partner recommended to the Foundation (a common 'pathway after training' that local partners adapt and apply, common outcome indicators and regional case exchange). The most persuasive argument pairs supply-side AI safety measures, such as model testing, with a demand-side measure: users' capability to use AI responsibly in their everyday decisions.

**The preconditions** are confirmation of what the funders (Google.org, AVPN and ADB) will allow; an explicit statement of the principles of ASEAN ownership, working through member states, openness and technology neutrality; and agreement on the Foundation's role in the Singapore follow-up work, both internally and with the LIP and partners. **The next decision point** is the Manila Summit in November 2026. At stake are the possible adoption of the 2027 ASEAN–China Year of AI Cooperation and the leaders' declaration on AI-driven MSME growth being pursued by the Philippines as ASEAN Chair.

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This paper maps ASEAN–China AI cooperation by AI layer and examines where, by what route and under what conditions the ASEAN Foundation's AI programmes (AI Ready ASEAN and AIM ASEAN) can contribute.

- **Key questions**: (1) How far has ASEAN–China AI cooperation progressed, and at which layers? (2) Does the cooperation address the capabilities of people and businesses that use AI, and particularly use after training? (3) Which of the Foundation's AI assets can it offer, and on what conditions?
- **Scope**: The paper covers only agreements, institutions and programmes directly related to AI. Trade (ACFTA 3.0) and general digital and education cooperation are mentioned only insofar as they concern AI.
- **Sources**: Official documents, press releases and media reports published up to 14 October 2026, supplemented by two internal reports from the local implementing partner of the Singapore National Policy Convening (review notes and the final report). The extent to which the internal reports may be disclosed is to be confirmed before they are cited externally. Closed-door consultations are not reflected.
- **Limits of assessment**: In the text, "not confirmed" means "not found in public sources"; it does not prove absence.
- **Notation**: Confirmed facts are distinguished from interpretation. Interpretations are marked **[Assessment]**, and anything that could not be established from sources is identified as such in the text.

This paper is background material for the ASEAN Foundation's mission to China in October 2026 and provides the rationale for the CIIS roundtable talking points (a separate document).

## 2. The ASEAN Foundation's AI Programmes

The Foundation's AI programmes rest on two pillars: AI Ready ASEAN focuses on people's AI literacy, and AIM ASEAN on AI adoption by MSMEs. Both are linked to the AI Opportunity Fund: Asia-Pacific (supported by Google.org and the ADB), which is run by AVPN.

### 2.3 AI Assets: Delivery, Content and Evidence

Three AI assets underpin the Foundation's contribution. Of these, the scarcest in discussions of AI cooperation is the third: field data on AI readiness.

1. **Delivery (AI trainer network)**: Under AI Ready ASEAN, more than 3,000 Master Trainers and a local implementing partner (LIP) in each member state; under AIM ASEAN, nine local partners. These are the channels that carry AI training all the way to schools, local communities and small businesses.
2. **Content (including AI Class ASEAN)**: AI Class ASEAN, a free AI learning platform, was launched in August 2025. It offers 25 topics and 70 modules in member states' languages, including modules on AI ethics, privacy and misinformation, and features an AI chatbot and real-time translation. Sources differ on the number of languages supported: the announcement gives 10, the platform lists 9. Under AIM ASEAN, local partners develop AI training courses for MSMEs in each country.
3. **Evidence (AI readiness data)**: AI Ready ASEAN Research (2026) found that 72.4 per cent of students and 70.5 per cent of educators had never taken an AI course. Educators' measured AI literacy fell short of their confidence, while students overestimated their own skills. Institutional readiness was lowest, and the urban–rural gap was also pronounced. On the MSME side, the National Policy Convenings in Singapore and Indonesia pointed to a gap between awareness and use of AI, uncertainty about where to start and where to apply it, a shortage of 'AI-bilingual' talent, and gaps in digital and financial literacy. For Singapore, the local implementing partner's internal report sets out structured findings (see 2.4).

**[Assessment]** The Foundation is one of the few institutions to hold both kinds of evidence: research that measures the AI readiness of teachers and students, and field records of the barriers MSMEs face in adopting AI, gathered through policy dialogues in member states. It can therefore put forward evidence on what AI cooperation programmes should teach, and to whom.

**[Assessment]** These findings matter for three reasons. First, they document from the field that, even in an ecosystem with strong policy and support, the bottleneck lies 'after training'. Second, the role the Foundation would play (a common pathway, outcome indicators, case exchanges) is set out in the partner's recommendations (subject to agreement), so the contribution it would offer the Chinese side is not an abstract proposal but builds on follow-up work the partner has already proposed. Third, the record that there was no single preference for any type of model provides field evidence for the principle of technology neutrality. As the report itself points out, however, Singapore has a strong institutional ecosystem and is geographically small. Any generalisation must also draw on results from other countries, such as Indonesia.

### 2.5 Limitations: What to Resolve Before Presenting Externally

- **Distinguishing reach from learning outcomes**: '5 million reached' and '100,000 in-depth course completions' are different indicators. Phase 1 targeted in-depth training for 800,000 people through Master Trainers. Whether the two figures are defined in the same way needs to be checked; where definitions are ambiguous, counterparts will discount the results as a whole. The Singapore LIP report likewise recommended distinguishing reach, business integration and verified outcomes in regional reporting.
- **Shift in target groups**: Phase 2 of AI Ready ASEAN centres on teachers and universities. AI literacy for parents and vulnerable groups is left to the legacy of Phase 1 (content and trainers).
- **Member state coverage**: Both programmes were designed for 10 member states. Timor-Leste became the 11th member state in October 2025, but Tetum-language AI content and local partners there are not confirmed.
- **Neutrality of funding and AI tools**: Both programmes rely on private foundations and funds, and the announcement of AIM ASEAN's local partners (October 2025) includes the phrase "use of Google's suite of AI tools". In cooperation with the Chinese side, the first question could be which AI tools are used for teaching (section 6).
- **Scope of disclosure for internal material**: The structured evidence for the Singapore findings is the LIP's internal report (submission date of the final version not yet fixed; it includes participants' names and the event budget). External documents and remarks should draw on it only to the extent covered by public press releases, or in the form of an anonymised summary.

**[Assessment]** Four points stand out from this map.

1. **China offers AI as a package.** It puts everything on the table at once, from infrastructure through industrial application and talent to standards and norms. ASEAN has its governance documents in place, but platforms on the Chinese side lead most of the implementation.
2. **The gap is 'the people who use AI'.** Talent programmes centre on officials, engineers and international students. For teachers, citizens and MSMEs, there are only courses run by Chinese companies and platforms, plus Smart Education, whose target audience has not been disclosed; no ASEAN-led, technology-neutral AI capacity programme is linked to the cooperation. How participants will be supported in applying AI after training, and how outcomes will be measured, have not been disclosed either. The Foundation's two programmes sit in this gap.
3. **In the governance and safety layer, the Foundation's contribution is 'user-side safety'.** It pairs supply-side measures, such as model testing and standards, with a demand-side one: the ability to understand AI and use it critically. The CIIS roundtable (Track 1.5) is a venue for making this case in the Foundation's own voice. As CIIS sits under China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, remarks should be prepared on the assumption that they will be passed on to the Chinese government.
4. **Now is the design stage.** Details of the AI ethics and safety initiative, the training courses at the China–ASEAN AI Industry Innovation Centre (Beijing) and Smart Education have yet to be made public. The next decision point is the November 2026 Summit and whether the 2027 ASEAN–China Year of AI Cooperation is adopted. If it is, both sides will be looking for AI cooperation deliverables for 2027.

### 3.3 Background: The Two Sides' AI Strategies

**ASEAN: non-binding AI governance and phased implementation.** The pillars are the ASEAN Guide on AI Governance and Ethics (February 2024), the Expanded ASEAN Guide on AI Governance and Ethics: Generative AI (January 2025), the ASEAN [Responsible AI Roadmap 2025–2030](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/27928-asean-responsible-ai-roadmap-2025-2030-was-adopted) (adopted March 2025), WG-AI and ASEAN AI Safe (Leaders' Declaration, October 2025; secretariat in Kuala Lumpur). In the [Hanoi Digital Declaration](https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/ADOPTED-HANOI-DIGITAL-DECLARATION_14Jan2026-CLN.pdf) of January 2026, ASEAN agreed on AI policy harmonisation and collective safety through WG-AI and ASEAN AI Safe. All of these are non-binding, and member states implement them at their own pace. ASEAN AI Safe is an internal ASEAN mechanism, not an ASEAN–China cooperation programme.

**China: AI capacity building as a pillar of its diplomacy towards developing countries.** In its [AI Capacity-Building Action Plan for Good and for All](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/mfa_eng/wjbzhd/202409/t20240927_11498465.html) (September 2024), China set out five goals: infrastructure, industry (AI+), talent development, data and safety. It followed with the Global AI Governance Action Plan in July 2025 and, in July 2026, the establishment of WAICO and 5,000 AI training opportunities. An ISEAS analysis identifies three channels for China–ASEAN AI exchanges: research centres and laboratories, policy forums, and training programmes. East Asia Forum (July 2026) argues that ASEAN should strike a balance by sourcing from multiple AI ecosystems and investing in its own capabilities.

**[Assessment]** For China, AI capacity building is an instrument tied to spreading its AI industry and standards. When ASEAN bodies contribute, they should therefore settle first what is off the table (learner data, lock-in to a particular AI platform), as much as what they put on it. By foregrounding alignment with ASEAN's non-binding AI governance frameworks, they can contribute while ASEAN retains the lead in the cooperation.

## 4. Gap Analysis: Who Cannot Use AI, and Where Support Breaks Off

ASEAN–China AI cooperation programmes are weighted towards building AI (models and standards) and deploying it (infrastructure and industrial applications). Programmes aimed at those who use AI, namely AI literacy for teachers and citizens and AI adoption capacity for MSMEs, could not be found in public sources. This group is not, however, an 'empty space': Chinese companies and platforms already provide AI education. Defined precisely, the gap is 'a space where ASEAN-led, technology-neutral public goods for AI capacity are not linked to the cooperation'. A second gap overlaps with this one: the stretch between receiving training and applying AI in real work (see 4.2).

### 4.2 Where It Breaks Off: The Gap after Training

The Singapore Convening viewed MSME adoption of AI as passing through the stages of awareness, experimentation, implementation and scaling, and found that support breaks off between them (see 2.4). Mapping current ASEAN–China cooperation and Foundation entry points against each stage gives the following picture.

| Stage | Barriers identified in Singapore | Support needed (the report's policy directions) | ASEAN–China cooperation today | Foundation entry points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Experience of using chatbots mistaken for readiness; unclear case for investment | Sector-specific demonstrations that start from business problems | Training and seminars: the 5,000 sessions and China–ASEAN AI Industry Innovation Centre (Beijing) training; Digital Academy scholarships | AI Class ASEAN; AIM ASEAN introductory courses |
| Experimentation | Uncertain use cases; fragmented data; opportunity cost of failure | Low-risk pilots using synthetic data and ready-made workflows; estimating total costs | Catalogue of AI application scenarios (more than 1,000) and company matchmaking at the China–ASEAN AI Application Cooperation Centre (Nanning); pilot support for MSMEs not confirmed | Sector-specific demonstration materials; guidance on responsible use |
| Implementation | Lack of in-house capacity; staff anxiety; integration and security costs | Solution signposting and referral desks; implementation support; workforce transition (AI + X, internal champions) | Could not be found in public sources | Local partner network; clinics after training (proposed in Singapore) |
| Scaling | Support ending when one-off workshops and short pilots finish; lack of evidence of results | Measuring sustained use and business value; delivery through trusted networks | Could not be found in public sources | Common outcome indicators; regional exchange of cases (recommended in Singapore) |

**[Assessment]** ASEAN–China talent programmes are concentrated on the first stage (awareness and basic training) and on high-level talent (engineers and officials); support for the implementation and scaling stages could not be found in public sources. The more a programme's targets are framed as training counts, the more likely it is to suffer the same break. The most distinctive contribution the Foundation can offer lies in these latter two stages.

**Risks from misuse of AI: a demand-side problem**

- ASEAN Digital Outlook 2026 identifies AI-generated threats, disinformation and scams as cross-border risks, and presents cybersecurity governance and digital literacy as two mutually complementary pillars (based on its summary).
- A representative of the ASEAN Business Advisory Council (ASEAN-BAC) warned that MSMEs are particularly vulnerable because scammers use deepfakes and voice cloning to impersonate customers and business partners (as reported by Eco-Business). **[Assessment]** There are three gaps: in target groups (the capacity of teachers, citizens and MSMEs to use AI); in stages (support and measurement for implementation and scaling after training); and in coordination (between dispersed AI channels on the Chinese side and existing AI programmes on the ASEAN side). Since both sides have made AI safety a major agenda item (ASEAN AI Safe; the September 2026 initiative on AI ethics and safety cooperation), the most persuasive argument pairs supply-side measures, such as model testing and standards, with demand-side measures: users' AI literacy and responsible use. ## 5. How the Foundation Can Contribute

The recommendation runs on two tracks.

**The immediate track is MSMEs.** Everything is already in place: evidence (the record of the Singapore Convening), an institutional basis (AIM ASEAN, endorsed by ACCMSME), proposed follow-up tasks (common outcome indicators and case exchange, subject to partner agreement) and opportunities (the CIIS industry session and the declaration of the Philippine-chaired summit in November). The Foundation presents A3, A4, B4, D1 and C5 together under a single message: 'we take on what comes after training'.

**The structural track is teachers.** Teachers' AI competence (C1) ties directly into Phase 2 of AI Ready ASEAN, endorsed by SOM-ED, but the target groups of China's Smart Education have not been made public, so exploration must come first. If teachers are included, the timing is right to prevent duplication now; if not, C1 shifts to complementarity, filling a gap (a proposal to link up at the teacher level). Either way, the Foundation reports to SOM-ED before proceeding.

**[Assessment] Why MSMEs are the first priority.** First, the evidence on the MSME side is more specific and more recent. Second, partners have recommended the role the Foundation would play (subject to agreement). Third, AIM ASEAN is scheduled to end in 2027, so securing a follow-up link is urgent. Fourth, C1 rests on a premise not yet confirmed (whether Smart Education covers teachers).

On this visit, Session 2 of the CIIS roundtable is the first opportunity to act. The meeting with the Ministry of Education will go no further than introducing A1 and A2 and confirming Smart Education's target groups and the channel for consultation. Both tracks share the same logic: ASEAN has the networks that carry AI through to the ground, and the evidence of how it is used after training.

### 5.1 Principles

1. **ASEAN ownership**: Ownership and branding of AI education content, trainer networks and AI readiness data rest with ASEAN.
2. **Through member states**: Education work goes through SOM-ED and member states' Ministries of Education, and MSME work through ACCMSME and member states' ministries responsible for MSMEs. In digital and AI governance, proposals go to ADGSOM and WG-AI via the ASEAN Secretariat.
3. **Openness**: The same proposals remain open to other Dialogue Partners. Nothing is designed as a China-only programme.
4. **Technology neutrality**: Nothing is made conditional on adopting AI models or platforms from a particular country or company. Education centres on competences rather than tools (judging where to use AI, verifying results, using it responsibly). The Singapore Convening, too, showed no single preference for any type of model.
5. **Complementarity**: The Foundation does not replace China's AI programmes; it concentrates on gaps in target groups (teachers, citizens, MSMEs), in stages (after training) and in coordination (preventing duplication).
6. **Speak through outcomes**: Reach (number of people trained), business integration and verified outcomes are reported separately. There is no assumption that every enterprise needs AI in every function.

### 5.3 Priorities

**The first action is A3.** It costs nothing, and the Foundation has already been invited. Framed by the 'after training' question, it presents the lessons from Singapore at a level that can be made public and raises the need for pathway design, a wider talent scope and measurement of sustained use (with A1 covered in the Q&A). As A4, B4, C5 and D1 have not yet been reported to ACCMSME, they are mentioned only as the Foundation's view of the problem and as tasks under consideration, not as concrete cooperation proposals. The structural priorities are as follows.

1. **MSME track (D1, A4, B4 → C5)**: Its timing matches the Singapore follow-up plan (baseline Q4 2026, review Q1 2027), and it is the role partners have recommended for the Foundation. Measurement (D1) and cases (A4) must be built up first to give C5 evidence to put forward. China, which has set a target of 5,000 AI training opportunities, also needs to demonstrate outcomes, so receptiveness is likely to be high **[Assessment]**.
2. **Teacher track (C1, with A1 and A2 as the entry point)**: It links directly to a programme endorsed by SOM-ED. The Foundation proceeds after confirming whether Smart Education covers teachers and reporting to SOM-ED.
3. **B1 and B2**: Both are low-cost and deliver quick results. B2 is the most direct way to add user-side content to both sides' AI safety agendas. Consultation with funders and a review of terms of use must come first.
4. **C2**: It responds most directly to the gaps but requires negotiation and funding project by project, so it starts with 1–2 pilots.
5. **C3 and C4**: C3 depends on the Year of AI Cooperation being adopted. C4 is an internal ASEAN priority, and neutrality shows most clearly when it is designed around multiple Dialogue Partners.

**Where C5 fits.** It is the end point of the MSME track. As AIM ASEAN is scheduled to end in 2027, follow-up funding will be discussed alongside it, with the aim of starting in the first half of 2027, depending on whether the MSME declaration is adopted at the November summit.

**The limits of B3.** The language-model layer is not the Foundation's core business. Its contribution is confined to experience of AI education in local languages and to terminology; it does not release learner data for model training.

### 5.4 What the Foundation Will Not Do

- Transferring or jointly holding learners' personal data and learning data, or providing data for AI model training
- Linkages conditional on adopting AI models or platforms from a particular country or company
- Repackaging ASEAN assets under the counterpart's programme brands
- School-level programmes run directly, bypassing member states' Ministries of Education
- Stating ASEAN positions on AI standards or norms (beyond the Foundation's mandate)
- Using internal reports or information on participating companies in external materials without the consent of the LIP and the companies
- Presenting follow-up plans still subject to partner agreement as if they were Foundation commitments

## 6. Preconditions and Risks
